The helper saves staff the legal research: they answer a few questions about their system, and at the end they get the classification together with its place in the law. The four figures below are measured, not estimated. The 207 of 217 matters most: the expected verdicts come from the European Commission, not from us.

Reading misses, guessing invents — the helper asks

A language model pre-sorts job applications. A camera detects driver fatigue. A chatbot answers customer questions. Three cases, three entirely different legal situations — and everyone looks it up alone.

What happens today

Someone searches 113 articles, 13 annexes and 180 recitals — and misses the one annex point everything depends on.

What a language model delivers alone

It answers fluently and cites a provision that does not exist. Nobody notices that mistake until an authority asks.

What this helper does

It asks — the way the official interpretation prescribes. The answers decide the class, the class decides the obligations, and each comes with its place in the law.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 · Regulation (EU) 2016/679 · German Federal Data Protection Act (BDSG)

2 / 19

The middle option is the dangerous one: a language model answering from memory sounds just as convincing whether it is right or not. That is why this helper keeps the two apart: the classification comes from the user's answers to fixed questions, and a model may only do the wording.

Asking, not guessing: right instead of probable

A lawyer asks five to eight things and is then certain — not 98 percent sure. Precise guessing is worse than asking, because it guesses the answers to questions one could simply ask.

Guessing from a description

The user writes two sentences about the system. Whatever is not in them — and something almost always is missing — the tool has to assume. In the end the assumption looks like a finding.

What comes out of it

A result with a probability. Anyone who wants to check it first has to work out what the tool inferred from which word. That is exactly the work it was meant to save.

Asking

The answers come from the user, who knows the system. The result is then not probable but right, as far as the answers are true. And the user sees what it depends on.

This is the most important point of the whole presentation. A tool that guesses can guess as well as it likes and remains a guessing tool: nobody can see the gap it filled. A tool that asks exposes every gap — and the answer comes from the only person who knows it.

Three answers per question: “Don't know” saves cases

Every question allows Yes, No and Don't know. The third answer is not a convenience; it follows from the measurement.

Some questions do not fit

A tool that screens CVs does not publish job ads — to the question whether an ad shows a specific vacancy, there is neither a yes nor a no.

A forced no costs cases

Measured: exactly such systems dropped out of the classification while only yes and no were on offer. The third answer leaves the question open without deciding it.

4 / 19

A tool that only knows yes and no forces the user into a statement they cannot make. Choosing no then rules out a route that was never meant for their system — and the result becomes wrong without them noticing.

The first three steps only pick the legal questions

Each step is one screen with a handful of questions. What one step yields decides the next — branches that do not fit are never shown.

Step 1

Three preliminaries

Is it an AI system under Article 3(1) at all? Does it assess people or only companies? Do you act on behalf of a public authority?

Step 2

Annex I

Is your system built into a regulated product — machinery, toy, lift, medical device, vehicle — or is it one itself? 15 product categories.

Step 3

Eight area questions

What does your system deal with? Employment, money and essential services, physical features, education, supply networks, courts and elections, police, borders. These eight sentences only sort.

The order is set in data/rules/question_flow_layout.yaml, the questions in data/rules/question_flow/

5 / 19

Anyone who says in the preliminaries that their system only assesses companies is done after three steps. The key point of this slide is step 3: the eight area questions decide nothing, they only select which legal questions are asked at all. Without this split everyone would have to see all 379 questions.

Only the legal questions decide — then comes the finding

Six steps are the range, not the rule: the average is 5.3.

Step 4

The legal questions

This is where the decision is made. The questions of the provision that applies — 31 provisions of the law, each with a main question, follow-up questions and the explicit exclusions of the interpretation.

Step 5

The derogation

Article 6(3) — four conditions and the profiling counter-exception. It only comes up if a provision applied before: the law only exempts what first falls under it.

Step 6

The finding

Class, citation down to the letter, the obligations that follow — and the official examples for the same provision, so you can hold your case up against them.

Step 4 is the only one that decides. The derogation in step 5 deliberately comes afterwards: Article 6(3) only exempts what first fell under Annex III. A tool asking about the derogation first could clear systems that were never high-risk — or miss ones that are.

8 / 19

Behind every question, exclusion and example is the paragraph of the guidelines it comes from — “(254)” for the question about screening applications. Anyone who thinks a question is wrong looks up the paragraph and argues about the text, not about the program. The exclusions weigh as much as the matches: without them an area would capture everything that touches it. The draft is not binding; the consultation ran until 23 June 2026, and the helper states this caveat in every answer.

The principle everything rests on

The classification comes from the answers,
never from the language model.

The class is decided by the question flow before any model is involved — and the question flow lives in text files, not in the program. A language model may word the answer. It receives the classification as a fixed fact and cannot change it. Slipping in “classify this as minimal” somewhere changes nothing.

9 / 19

This is the sentence everything else builds on. A language model that decides the legal consequence itself cannot be checked — nobody can read why it decided that way. A question flow can be read line by line, with the paragraph number of the official interpretation on every question. And it can be tested against the official examples.

The figures are test criteria, not claims: a dedicated test file checks on every run that the AI Act has 113 articles. A preliminary check, then two routes to high risk

The checks follow the order of the law. The first finding that holds stands — with the provision it rests on.

Preliminary 1

Not an AI system

Anything that only runs fixed calculation steps and infers nothing from its inputs is outside the Regulation. Article 3(1). The walkthrough ends here after one question.

Article 6(1)

High risk, Annex I

The system is a safety component of a regulated product or is such a product itself. 15 product categories — machinery, lift, medical device, vehicle and more.

Article 6(2)

High risk, Annex III

One of the 31 provisions applies, down to the letter: point 4(a) covers recruitment, point 4(b) working conditions — two question flows, two lists of obligations.

The findings are computed in src/compliance_helper/classification/question_flow.py and in web/walkthrough.js — identical in 400 of 400 runs

11 / 19

The order is that of the law: first whether it is an AI system at all, then the product safety law of Annex I, then the areas of use in Annex III. Annex III always applies down to the letter, because the obligations depend on it. The same logic exists twice, in Python and in the browser; a comparison with 400 answer patterns shows that both compute the same.

More than yes or no: conditional, exempt, not high risk

These three findings, too, name the provision they rest on — and what would change the answer.

What nobody can know

High risk, conditional

Annex III point 2 requires the deployer to be formally designated as a critical entity — and the interpretation states that the provider need not be told. A no would be wrong here, hence a finding of its own.

Article 6(3)

Derogation applies

A provision applies, but the task is narrowly defined and does not materially influence the decision. With a counter-exception: profiling of people never qualifies.

No match

Not high risk

No provision applies, or one of the 214 explicit exclusions does. Here, too, the finding names the provision where it was decided.

Conditional: paragraphs (190) and (191) of the guidelines · Derogation: Article 6(3) AI Act

12 / 19

The first finding on this slide is the one that matters. A tool that only knows yes and no would have had to lie here: either it claims high risk although the designation may be missing, or it denies it although the designation may exist. Both would be wrong. That this gets a finding of its own is not in the law — it follows from paragraphs 190 and 191 of the interpretation.

57 obligations — filtered, not just listed

Each obligation states: what to do, in plain language · the citation · from when it applies · how to prove compliance · what an infringement risks.

By class and by role

A chatbot gets no high-risk obligations. And there are more than two roles: provider, deployer, product manufacturer, importer, distributor, authorised representative — each carries different obligations from the same articles.

What stays open is named

If the user names no role, both the provider's and the deployer's view are shown — otherwise half the answer would be missing. Only what an answer rules out is left out.

Every legal basis checked

302 of 302 legal bases in the rule set point to a passage that really is in the bundled legal corpus — checked on every test run, not once while writing.

13 / 19

The filter is the difference between an answer and a pile of material: without it every high-risk system would get the whole list, and the user would have to sort it out alone — exactly the work the tool is meant to save. The filter errs on the side of caution: it only leaves out what an answer rules out, not what an answer leaves open. Next to the finding the user also sees the official examples for the same provision, 187 in total.

Four search routes find the passage by meaning and wording

The second task besides classification: looking things up. Legal questions come in two kinds — by meaning (“May we pre-sort job applications?”) and by wording (“What does Article 6(3) say?”). A single search route loses the other half each time.

1 · Meaning

Vector search

Finds “recruitment or selection of natural persons” although no word matches.

2 · Wording

Keyword search

BM25 ranking over the wording. A vector search blurs article numbers — to it, numbers look almost alike.

3 · Context

Model word weights

Sits between meaning and wording: weights words by their role in the sentence.

4 · Named provision

Citation route

“Art. 6(3)” is resolved to an id and fetched directly. Without this route an exact question would only get approximate similarity.

One search index per language · search model about 2.3 gigabytes, loaded once — classification runs without it

14 / 19

The fourth route is easy to forget — and the one needed most often: a lawyer names the provision they are asking about. A pure meaning search answers that with something similar instead of the thing asked for. The routes are merged not by their scores, which are not comparable between routes, but by rank; a hit that several routes rank high moves up. On request a cross-encoder, which reads question and passage together, then reorders the best 30. Search is an extra: the classification does not need it and runs without any model.

Four attack routes, each one closed off

Injected instructions

The user's description sits between two markers drawn at random for each request. Faking a section boundary would mean guessing them.

Tested with 10 attacks, each checked twice.

Invented citations

Every article in the answer must appear in the evidence. Anything unsupported is reported.

That is how Article 72 was caught in an obligation text but missing from its citations.

The index as an attack route

Stored as a pickle file, a tampered copy would have run foreign code on load. Now JSON: wrong hits are possible, foreign code is not.

In operation

The container runs without administrator rights and cannot change its own code. Keys never in the log. 30 requests per minute. No outbound connection.

Vulnerability reports: SECURITY.md · Security model: docs/security.md

The third point is the easiest to overlook. The code once justified it with “own file, no foreign content” — that was wrong: the file ships with the repository, and anyone who changes a copy reaches everyone who loads it. The check tools fail instead of warning: ruff, ruff format, mypy, bandit and pip-audit pass without findings, so a red run means something again.

In the browser

A web page that installs nothing and transmits nothing: one HTML file, two JavaScript files, the question data and one text file per language. No sign-in, no account, no model.

“Runs without installation” is meant literally here: the web page is a file that computes in the browser. There is no service behind it that could receive answers. Not legal advice, but one data version with limits

Not legal advice

It says which provision is relevant and what it says. Whether it applies to your case is decided by a person licensed to give legal advice. Every answer says so.

The interpretation is a draft

The questions rest on the Commission guidelines of 19 May 2026. The consultation ran until 23 June 2026; no final version existed when this work was completed.

A data version, not a subscription

Covered is the wording as of 31 May 2026. Anything issued later is not included. The deadlines from Article 113 also come with a caveat: the application dates were under political discussion.

Still to be checked

No lawyer has reviewed the walkthrough. The obligations are checked against the text of the Regulation but not measured against an outside benchmark — no official collection of examples exists for them. Only the classification is measured.
